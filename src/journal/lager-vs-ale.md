Nearly every beer ever brewed falls into one of two families. Not by colour, not by
strength, and not by how it looks in the glass — by yeast.

Once that distinction clicks, a confusing wall of taps turns into something you can
actually navigate.

## One difference, and it is the yeast

**Ales** are made with yeast that works warm, somewhere around 15–24°C, and gathers
near the top of the vessel as it ferments. Brewers call it top-fermenting. It works
fast, often finishing in a few days.

**Lagers** use yeast that works cold, nearer 7–13°C, and settles to the bottom.
Then the beer rests cold for weeks. *Lagern* is German for "to store", and the
storing is the whole idea.

That is the entire distinction. Everything else follows from it.

## What the temperature does to the taste

Warm yeast is busy yeast. Along with alcohol it throws off esters and phenols —
compounds that read as fruit and spice. The banana-and-clove note in a German wheat
beer is not an added flavouring; it is the yeast, warm and left alone.

Cold yeast is quieter. It produces far fewer of those compounds, and the long cold
rest lets the rough edges settle out. What is left is the malt and the hops, with
very little between you and them.

Hence the shorthand: **ales taste of fermentation, lagers taste of ingredients.**

It also explains why lagers are harder to brew well. There is nowhere to hide. An
ale's yeast character will paper over a small flaw; a pilsner will show you
everything.

## Two things that are not the difference

**Colour.** The most common misreading. Guinness is a dark ale, but Munich dunkel
and schwarzbier are dark *lagers*. Meanwhile plenty of ales are pale gold. Colour
comes from how the malt was kilned — nothing to do with the yeast.

**Strength.** Ales are not inherently stronger. A [strong lager](/strong) can sit
comfortably above many ales, and English session bitters are often weaker than the
lager in your fridge. Strength comes from fermentable sugar, not family.

## Hybrids, for completeness

A few styles sit awkwardly between the families, because brewers enjoy
complicating things. Kölsch is fermented warm like an ale and then cold-conditioned
like a lager. German altbier does much the same. California common runs
lager yeast at ale temperatures.

They are the exceptions that prove the rule — and they are worth seeking out
precisely because they refuse to pick a side.

## The short version

Warm yeast, top of the vessel, fast, fruity and expressive: **ale**. Cold yeast,
bottom of the vessel, slow, clean and precise: **lager**. Colour and strength tell
you nothing about which is which.

That is genuinely most of what there is to it. The rest is just drinking enough of
both to know which you reach for — and you can see where to find ours on the
